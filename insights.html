Beyond pay: what professionals told us

Our 2026 survey of BD, marketing and communications professionals asked about more than pay. Three findings stand out for anyone running or building a team.

Read the article

Most salary research stops at salary. Ours asked business development, marketing and communications professionals across law, accounting, engineering and consulting what else shapes their decisions, and the answers say more about retention than any pay table.

Open, but not moving

77% are at least open to a move in the next twelve months, and 18% are actively looking. Yet only 15% actually changed firm in the past year, and confidence in the job market averages 5.4 out of ten, with half the profession at five or below. That is a lot of people standing near the door without walking through it. When confidence turns, the moves will come quickly, and the firms that have invested in their people will keep them.

What actually moves people

Pay still tops the list, as it always has. What matters is what sits behind it. Career progression is ranked a top three reason to move by 53%, then firm culture at 35%, flexibility at 34% and the culture of the BD and marketing team itself at 33%. Title trails at 21%.

Now look at the bottom of the chart. The things firms lead with in their advertising, the brand and the technology investment, are ranked a top three factor by 4%. People do not join a logo or a tech stack. They join a path upwards and a team they want to sit with.

Promotion is a retention decision

42% are actively seeking promotion, and 27% of the whole profession say they would leave to get one. Only 15% are content at their current level. Every promotion round is therefore a retention event: the people you pass over are not just disappointed, a quarter of the market has already decided what they will do about it.

The market is coiled: three in four people are open, one in four would leave over promotion, and almost nobody has moved yet.

The BD hire who can coach partners

Training partners to sell only sticks when someone inside the firm reinforces it daily. That is a hiring profile, and it is rare.

Read the article

Most firms have paid for sales training for partners at some point. Most will also admit that six months later, little had changed. External coaching creates a moment. Behaviour changes when someone inside the firm keeps the standard alive between moments.

The profile

The people who do this well are senior BD leaders who have earned the right to challenge: they have sat in enough pitches, won enough work and read enough rooms that partners take the feedback. They coach in the flow of real pursuits, not in workshops. And they are comfortable being the only person in the building whose job is to say the practice run was not good enough.

How to interview for it

Ask for the last time they changed how a partner sells, what the partner did differently, and what it won. Candidates who can answer in specifics are the ones who have actually done it. Titles do not predict this skill. Scar tissue does.

Buy training for a moment. Hire the person who makes it a habit.

Commercial BD is on the rise

Commercially focused BD roles are on the rise, dramatically. Here is why it is happening, and how to position for one.

Read the article

Every recruiter I speak to is seeing the same trend

Put the specialist BD recruiters in a room and we would argue about plenty. Not this. The briefs on our desks have all changed in the same direction.

Five years ago the typical search was a BD or marketing manager, a capable generalist supporting a practice group. Today it is pursuits, client development, pricing, key accounts or growth. The word commercial is in almost every brief.

Waves of client facing roles are here, and more are coming, because firms are finally ready: the support layer is gradually being filled with AI driven technology. That is where the headcount, the budget and the pay rises are going.

The market is moving. The question is how you can make your role move with it.

Reason one: firms are paying for revenue, not reactivity

Partners have always been measured on origination. Firms now expect the same of the people around them. A BD function that cannot be tied to revenue is a cost line, and cost lines get reviewed.

The managing partners I speak to no longer ask whether the team is busy. They ask what the team won, what it protected and what it is worth. The roles being created answer that question: client directors, pursuit leads, pricing specialists, each close enough to the fee to be measured by it.

Firms increasingly want revenue you influenced pointed to.

Reason two: AI is taking the production work

Much of what filled a BD manager’s week was production: credentials, capability statements, first drafts of proposals, directory submissions, event collateral. AI is taking that work inside some leading firms right now, and not slowly.

Those roles are shrinking, and people feel uncomfortable about it. But the AI infrastructure development version of these roles is growing, for the people who are AI curious: the ones building the prompts, the workflows and the data the rest of the team will run on.

If you cannot use AI in the office, use it every day at home, including weekends. Spend the money on the top models, download the plugins, train your AIs. A $300 to $500 investment, and your spare time, is a critical investment in your future. It is mainly those sitting outside large organisations, without constraints, who see just how powerful it is.

Many people spend that on a weekend of dinner and drinks. Spend your weekends in, learning intensively and fast.

Reason three: clients buy differently, and private capital changed the language

General counsel and CFOs run panels, demand pricing certainty and move work between firms more readily than they did. Procurement sits in the room. The relationship still matters, but it no longer closes on its own.

At the same time the client group the top firms most want to grow is private capital: funds, their portfolio companies and their advisers. Winning that work means speaking the language of deals, returns and timing.

Both shifts create the same need: people who understand how the client makes money and how it buys. Firms are short of them.

Many clients are changing how they buy. Firms are hiring people who understand this.

Reason four: the titles give it away

Watch the job titles. Client development director. Head of pursuits. Pricing and commercial manager. Growth lead. Key account director. These were rare in Australian firms a few years ago. Now they are the senior roles.

On the strategic marketing side, business generation managers are being installed with a marketing and communications remit tied directly to proactive pursuit activity, not business as usual.

The old titles are still there, but they sit lower in the structure and are paid accordingly. The premium has moved to the people whose work can be measured against fees.

If you are a generalist, become as senior as you can by putting your hand up for projects, and operate as a highly data and AI fluent generalist. There is demand there, and it is building.

Commercial specialist or data and AI fluent generalist. Decide which you are, and be seen to be on it.

How to position: learn the numbers the partners watch

Commercial fluency starts with the firm’s own numbers: revenue, margin, utilisation, realisation, lock up, write offs. It is imperative to understand how the firm makes money.

Then look outward at what moves your clients. Bond yields. Capital flows. Right now that means watching US, Japanese and South Korean money into Australia, plus Canadian and Singaporean pension and sovereign funds, Middle East sovereign wealth and UK and European capital, and working out the second, third and fourth order effects downstream before that investment lands in a sector and the work follows it.

Partners are paid to spot that early. Walk in with a map of where the next instructions will come from and you are origination, not support.

Opportunity shows up in the capital flows before it shows up in the pipeline.

How to position: attach yourself to revenue and get in the room

Find one thing in your current role that touches the fee line and own it. A key client plan. A live pursuit. A pricing conversation. A panel bid. It does not need to be big. It needs to be measurable, and it needs a record you keep.

Then get client time. If you have never sat in a pitch, run a client feedback interview or joined a relationship review, you are describing the work second hand. Ask to be in the room. Partners rarely say no to someone who wants to hear what the client thinks.

Over a year the record and the client time become your case for the commercial role, written in outcomes rather than activities.

One client, one pursuit, one number. Build the evidence before you need it.

How to position: self educate intensively

Do not study an MBA. They are high value, but only to those who already hold them. A fresh start MBA is now too slow, and you can learn far more relevant material faster and cheaper.

Study selective modules instead. Short, recognised and cheap: the CFA Investment Foundations certificate or CFA Level I; the CAIA Fundamentals certificate or Level I, which will teach you private capital and private equity, exactly where firms are chasing growth; a graduate certificate in applied finance; a financial modelling course such as the FMVA; a legal pricing or project management certificate; and RG146 if your clients are funds or financial services firms.

Then fill the gaps with audiobooks on finance, markets and deals. Commutes, the gym, weekends. Understanding the client’s world is mostly self taught.

Before you fund a qualification, ask what commercial gap it closes. An MBA rarely closes one.

A final word

I have spent twenty years placing BD and marketing people into Australian law, accounting and engineering firms. I have never seen the market move this clearly in one direction. The firms are not waiting for the profession to catch up. The people who move first will set the pay for everyone else.

Commercial roles are being created in some firms, and those who are moving fast, win.

Could everyday AI use help BD teams win more work?

Deidentified and aggregated at firm level, the way teams already use AI could show what wins work, and where coaching would lift win rates.

Read the article

Most firms now have some view of how their people use AI tools day to day: which prompts get written, which documents get summarised, which pitches get drafted with help. Almost none of them have asked whether that data, deidentified and aggregated at firm level, could tell them something about how work gets won.

The hypothesis is simple. Teams that win more work probably use these tools differently from teams that win less. They may research the client more deeply before a meeting, test a pitch against a sceptical reader, or spend more time on the problem and less on the formatting. If that pattern exists, it is coaching material. It would show a BD leader where a lift in win rate is available and what it would take.

It has to be built the right way: lawful from the start, privacy and privilege protected, confidential client material kept out, and never used to assess individual employees. That last point is not a nicety. The moment people believe the data is being used to judge them, the behaviour changes and the signal is gone. Done properly, this is about firms learning, not monitoring.

There is also a hiring angle. The BD people who are already working this way, who treat the tools as a research and drafting partner rather than a shortcut, are visible in interview. They talk about the client’s problem first and the pitch second. They are the ones firms should be competing for.

Nobody has built this properly yet in Australian professional services, as far as I can see. The firm that does will learn more about its own BD function in a quarter than most learn in a decade.

Why the counter offer is usually the wrong answer

You resign, and suddenly there is money on the table that was not there at your last review. Before you take it, ask why it took a resignation to find it.

Read the article

A counter offer answers one question, pay, and leaves every other reason you started looking untouched. The partner who does not share credit, the structure that will not change, the promotion that keeps slipping a cycle. Those are still there on Monday.

It also changes how the firm sees you. You have shown you will leave. In my experience most people who accept a counter offer are looking again within a year, and this time the market knows it.

If the money matters, it should have been there before you resigned. A pay benchmark will tell you what the role pays elsewhere, so you can have that conversation early, from a position of knowledge, rather than at the door.

Take the counter offer if the only thing wrong was pay and the firm has fixed it properly. Otherwise, leave well, and keep the relationship.

Leaving well matters more than people think. Professional services is a small market, and the partner you resign from this year will be a client, a referee or a colleague again within a few years. Give proper notice, hand over cleanly, and do not use the counter offer as leverage with the new firm. Both sides will remember how it was done.

And if you are the firm making the counter offer, the honest question is why the money is suddenly available now. If the person was underpaid, that was known before they resigned. Fixing pay at the door buys a year at most. Fixing it at the review buys loyalty, and it is cheaper.

How to secure a pay rise in professional services

Firms do not pay for effort. They pay for a case. Here is how to build one that a partner can take to the remuneration committee.

Read the article

Start with the market, not with your feelings about it. Know the band for your level and your city, and where you sit inside it. A free pay benchmark takes ten minutes and gives you a number nobody can argue with.

Then build the case in the language the firm uses. Pitches won and their value. Clients retained. Panels secured. Revenue you can point to, with the partners who will back you. Write it down. Two pages, no adjectives.

Ask at the right time. Mid year reviews and the weeks before budgets are set are when there is money to move. Ask in the fortnight after a pitch you won, not the month before a round of redundancies.

Ask for a number, not a review. Give the firm the figure, the evidence and a date. If the answer is no, ask what would need to be true for it to be yes, and when. If there is no answer to that, you have your answer.

Separate pay from title. Firms will often offer a new title in place of money because it costs them nothing. A title is worth having if it changes what you can do and who you report to. It is worth nothing if it is a label on the same job. Take the title and keep asking for the number.

Finally, know your alternative before you ask. Not a bluff, a real view of what the market would pay you to move and how long it would take to find that role. That is where a confidential conversation with a recruiter earns its keep. You may never use it. Knowing it changes how you sit in the room.

The CV that gets read

Every application to Dalton Handley is read by Charles. Here is what he is looking for in the first thirty seconds, and what gets in the way.

Read the article

Lead with outcomes, not duties. A partner does not want to know you managed the pitch process. They want to know you won the $9m tender, or built the key client programme that added three panels in a year. Numbers, firms described if not named, dates.

Two pages. The first page should tell the whole story on its own: who you are, the firms, the level, the three things you are known for. The second page is evidence.

Cut the words that do everything and nothing. Passionate, dynamic, results driven, stakeholder management. Replace each with the thing you actually did.

And tell me what you want next. The CVs that get a call back are the ones where it is obvious what the right next role looks like, because that is the one I can go and find.

A few things that get a CV put down. Gaps with no explanation, when one line would do. A list of every system you have ever used. Three pages of duties that could describe anyone at your level in any firm. A photo. An objective statement that says you are seeking a challenging role in a dynamic environment. None of these are fatal on their own. Together they tell a partner you did not think about who would read it.

The best CVs are written for one reader: the person who will decide whether to meet you. They answer the three questions that reader has in the first thirty seconds. Can this person do the job? Have they done something like it before? Will the partners want them in front of clients? Everything that helps answer those stays. Everything else goes.

A $9m tender and a role that should not be filled as designed

Brief taken on Friday, role rebuilt over the weekend, operator secured within a week. Two days after she started, the tender landed.

Read the article

The brief arrived on a Friday. A national firm needed a senior BD person in place quickly, with a major tender expected and the previous incumbent gone. The role as written was a like for like replacement: a full time, permanent head of pursuits at a package that reflected the last person, not the work.

Reading the brief over the weekend, the problem was clear. The firm did not need a permanent head of pursuits. It needed an operator who could run one large tender to a very high standard, build a repeatable process around it and hand it over. That is a different role, with a different cost base and a different kind of person.

So the role was rebuilt. The permanent headcount came out, which removed a fixed cost the firm did not need. The delivery model changed to an interim engagement with a defined scope and a clear end point. The price was reset to reflect the work rather than the title. And the search was pointed at a short list of people who had actually run tenders of that size, not people who had managed teams that had.

The operator was secured within a week. Two days after she started, the tender landed. It came in at just over $9m.

The value was not filling the role quickly. It was recognising that the role should not be filled as originally designed: removing unnecessary fixed cost, changing the delivery model, repricing it and finding the right operator. That is what a recruiter should be doing with a brief, and it is the part most firms never see.

Attributed revenue is not portable client value

Big Law has become a market for portable revenue. Or, more accurately, for the promise of it. The better question is how much of it will actually move.

Read the article

A partner may arrive with a very strong business case. Significant billings. Deep client relationships. A loyal team. But revenue attribution is not always the same thing as client ownership.

Inside a firm, revenue gets attributed to whoever originated the matter, led the relationship or sat closest to the general counsel. Those are three different things, and only one of them travels. A client who instructed a firm because of its bench, its pricing or its panel position will often stay exactly where it is when the partner leaves.

The due diligence that matters is not the billing history. It is the client’s view. Who does the general counsel call when something goes wrong? Who sits on the panel, the partner or the firm? How many of the top ten relationships would survive a conflict check at the new firm? Those questions are rarely asked with any rigour, and the answers decide whether a lateral hire pays for itself.

This is where BD earns its place in the conversation. A good client programme knows the relationships at a level the partnership does not: who the secondary contacts are, which matters came through the panel rather than the partner, and where the firm itself is the brand. That information should sit in the lateral business case before the guarantee is signed.

The firms that benefit most from lateral hiring will not necessarily be those that spend the most. They will be the ones best at distinguishing between attributed revenue and truly portable client value.

Interviews are conversations, not exams

Preparation should never mean scripting answers. Understand the role, sharpen your proposition, and make it about the client’s problems.

Read the article

The candidates who interview worst are often the ones who prepared hardest, in the wrong way. They arrive with answers to the questions they expect, and they deliver them whether or not the question was asked. It reads as rehearsed because it is.

Preparation should never mean scripting answers. It should mean understanding the role, the firm and the person across the table well enough to have a real conversation with them. Who are their key clients? What has changed in their market this year? What would make the next twelve months a success for the person hiring you? If you cannot answer those going in, you have not prepared.

In the room, ask intelligent questions about the client and the problems they are trying to solve. Make it about them. Then bring in the relevant parts of your experience, much as a consultant would: here is a similar problem I have seen, here is what we did, here is what I would look at first in your case. That is how senior people talk to each other, and it is how a hiring partner decides you can sit in front of their clients.

Sharpen your proposition before you go. One or two sentences on what you are good at and the evidence for it. Numbers where you have them. Firms described if not named. Everything else in the conversation hangs off that.

And treat the interview as the first meeting of a working relationship, not an exam. The firm is deciding whether to work with you. You are deciding whether to work with them. Both of those are easier to judge in a conversation than in a test.

Hiring may be about to change. Here is what I am seeing.

Rates are going to rise and consumer confidence sits where it did in 2022. Hiring will not stop. It will concentrate, and the people who own a pipeline will be the ones in demand.

Read the article

I went through 370 data releases and more than 500 indicators, and modelled three paths for unemployment. None of them are great. There will be a hiring impact, but it will be uneven, and it will create opportunities.

Having watched this cycle three times from inside it, here is what happens in law and accounting. Firms start hiring selectively. Support function hiring slows before fee earners. Permanent BD and marketing hiring falls 15 to 25 percent over the next year, with coordinator, events and generalist roles slowing first.

The good news is in two parts. Law and accounting firms bill on activity, not on growth, and a slowing economy with rising rates and falling asset prices generates a great deal of activity. And demand does not disappear, it concentrates: at manager and senior manager level, with the BD people who own a practice group pipeline, run the pursuits and can talk pricing with a partner. Supply in that middle layer is thin, because firms promoted or lost their best senior managers between 2020 and 2023.

Hiring fell 9 percent year on year in professional services while job postings rose 14 percent. Firms are still looking. They are just taking longer to decide, and waiting longer before they come to a recruiter. The cost of an empty seat usually outweighs the fee.

Not a crisis. A market moving from rewarding movement to rewarding judgement.

Private equity in Australian law has been years in the making

The question is not whether an approach from private equity is attractive. It is what the firm wants capital to achieve, and what that asks of BD.

Read the article

Private equity interest in Australian law firms did not arrive overnight. The groundwork has been laid for years: alternative business structures, listed firms, consolidation in the mid market and a generation of partners who have watched accounting and advisory firms take outside capital and grow.

The question for a partnership is not whether an approach is attractive. Most are, on paper. The question is what the firm wants capital to achieve. Succession and a payout for retiring partners is one answer. Funding a technology build is another. Buying scale in a practice area or a new city is a third. Each of those asks something different of the firm, and of its BD and client function.

If investors are providing capital to accelerate growth, BD and client teams become central to the growth thesis. The question asked of them changes from what did we win to where should we invest next. That is a different job, with a different kind of person in it: someone who can read a market, build a case for a practice or a sector and be held to a number.

It also changes what the function reports on. Pipeline, conversion, share of panel spend and client profitability move from nice to have to board papers. Firms that already run their BD function that way will find the conversation with investors easy. Firms that still treat BD as a support service will be asked to rebuild it, usually at speed.

The hiring implication is simple. The BD leaders who have run a function inside a business with external shareholders, or who have worked alongside private capital, are suddenly worth more to a law firm than they were a year ago. There are not many of them in Australia.

Having worked across legal BD and marketing for many years, one thing has become increasingly clear: lawyers did not generate those outcomes alone. BD and marketing functions have helped drive sector strategies, client growth, pursuits, market positioning and increasingly the commercial infrastructure around revenue. Yet many firms still treat BD and marketing as one broad support category, grouped into banded salary frameworks moved largely through CPI linked increases and discretionary bonuses. That framing is becoming harder to sustain.

Not everyone in BD and marketing creates the same value. Some roles are operational. Others directly influence growth, partner behaviour, client development and market position. So the answer is not blanket salary increases. It is sharper differentiation around where value sits now and where it is likely to sit next.

This is where technological change starts to matter. Generative AI and automation are compressing large parts of the operational workload across pitches, credentials, research, submissions and reporting. The work is not disappearing, but the manual effort required to deliver it is falling. When productivity shifts that quickly it does not just change how work gets done. It changes how teams are built. As the cost of execution falls, the relative value of commercial judgement rises: people who can connect capability across functions, guide partners, shape sector strategy, lead pursuits and convert market intelligence into revenue.

That reshapes the traditional pyramid. Less weight at the base. More value concentrated through the middle and upper layers. Greater importance attached to judgement, orchestration and commercial credibility. Less pyramid, more diamond, not just in shape but in where firms assign value.

Some firms have been moving quietly in this direction for years. The two firms I have placed the most people into over nearly two decades also have the highest average tenure across their BD and marketing teams, at more than seven years. That sounds counterintuitive, because high placement volumes are usually associated with churn. It reflects something different: the distinction between growth firms and churn firms. Growth firms hire repeatedly because the work expands, people are promoted and new capability is added to a more senior weighted team. Roles are created rather than recycled. Both of those firms moved away from the pyramid some time ago.

The practical question for firms is where to invest: develop and retain the future senior operators already inside the team, or wait and compete for them externally once the market tightens. If more firms reallocate headcount in the same direction, demand will concentrate quickly around commercially minded operators with judgement, credibility and broad market perspective.

It also raises a less comfortable question. If a law firm only hires BD and marketing talent from strings of other law firms, why is that? If the answer is familiarity, partner comfort or safety in tradition, it may say more about internal assumptions than about where the best commercial talent sits. One of the more progressive firms I work with recently met a candidate whose background combined legal experience with time inside a major global consumer business. That breadth was precisely the attraction. Private capital, consolidation and scale may accelerate the shift. They will not create it. The more progressive firms have already started.

Tackling redundancy and re entering a competitive job market

Redundancy in a tight market is hard. It is not the end of a career. A practical guide for BD, marketing and communications people who find themselves out of work.

Read the article

Redundancy can strike as a harsh blow, especially in a competitive market. There are roles out there and I am working on them, but there can be dozens of candidates competing for some spots. In seventeen years as a specialist recruiter I have taken many difficult phone calls from people who unexpectedly found themselves out of work. The first thing I tell them is that they have lost a job, not their skills, and that the market turns. It always has.

Understand the market first. The years of strong firm revenue growth produced inflated salaries as firms bid for talent from a thin pool, and retention pay rises on top. When the cycle turns and redundancies swell the pool, the tables turn with it and salary expectations from two years earlier need to soften. Law firms are the engine room of professional services BD and marketing recruitment. Once a couple of them hire aggressively again, the game of musical chairs starts and movement spreads across the market.

Keep calm and carry on. That means a systematic approach to the problem: accept what you cannot change, work hard on what you can. Worrying about the mortgage, what people think, or your former employer clouds your thinking and slows you down. Clear thinking is the asset you need most.

Embrace constructive self criticism. Some redundancies happen where you were a number and great people are let go. Some are just unfortunate. Quite often, though, people who are made redundant have become a little stale without noticing. Consider what you did well and can take to a new employer, and be honest about what you could sharpen. Work out what you are not good at and work on it. Work out what you are good at and build on it.

Reconsider the roadmap. Rather than climbing the next rung, consider a role at the same level or slightly below in a firm with a more sophisticated function, where you will learn. For senior people, dropping the team management and the title can mean a senior specialist role that still challenges you. I know former heads of function who are now senior managers, and very happy ones.

Revamp the CV so it leads with outcomes. Consider contract roles: in professional services most contract roles convert to permanent, and they are often passed over by candidates waiting for security. Stay informed and network properly, with people you do not know well, offering something in return. Avoid the gossip. LinkedIn is your workbook and contact list.

Be patient and be prepared, psychologically and financially, for six months out of work if you are mid to senior level. Do not fire your CV at every role you see, including ones several grades below yours. You will look desperate, you will be seen as a flight risk, and when the right role comes up at that firm it will be obvious you have been on the market a while. Keep the line open with your recruiter instead, because the proactive searches often start before a role is advertised.

Use the time. Courses, certifications and interview practice are undervalued, and I have run interview technique sessions for over a decade because they change outcomes. Treat the time as a chance to realign your career with what you actually want, and to look at avenues you had not considered. Redundancy in a tepid market is challenging. It is not insurmountable, and the people who use the time well come back stronger.
